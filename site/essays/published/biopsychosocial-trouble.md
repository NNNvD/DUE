The biopsychosocial model remains one of the most influential models of disease. Proposed in 1977, the original paper has been cited over 25000 times, of which about 5000 citations in the last two years. It is a critique on the biomedical view that disease is, or can be, sufficiently explained by biological abnormalities and argues that social, psychological, and behavioral dimensions are integral to our assessment and understanding of illness and health. An extensive literature exists around the foundations of the model, contemporary revisions have been suggested, and criticisms range from philosophically inconsistent to too vague to be practical. Yet, its core message is retained in any textbook and (way too) many conference presentations.

My problem lies not with Engel’s view, but with the simplicity with which it is generally presented, and the implications researchers and clinicians seem to draw from this. Although the original paper contains no figures, and Engel’s later work on the clinical application of the model represents the systems as nested in each other, the common representation is that of three separate boxes or a Venn diagram with whatever needs explaining where everything overlaps. The take-home messages that seem to be drawn from this are that biology, psychology, and society are (partially) independent domains; that together they explain disease (or symptoms, or health, or whatever the topic of the presentation); and that the three are approximately equally important. The first is misleading, the second is so broad that it tells us very little, and the third is false.

In reality, psychological experiences and biological processes are different levels of description of the same thing: the individual. Anything psychological must necessarily have a biological correlate; most prominently every psychological state must necessarily have an accompanying brain state. This is not to ultimately reduce the one to the other or to fundamentally prefer lower-level explanations, it merely acknowledges these are levels within the same hierarchy. Separating psychological and biological explanations is useful and likely essential to our understanding of disease but how intimately they are connected should not be lost in the process.

A similar case can be made for society.  Arguably, and in line with Engel’s nesting, individuals make up society as much as biology and psychology make up individuals. In that sense, society too is another level of description. This seems to restore some equivalence between the domains, but there is a reason to pause at the person. It is a person who becomes ill, who feels pain, and who suffers. It is at the level of the person that we are trying to ultimately understand what is going on and it is for that reason that the distinction between psychological and biological is qualitatively different from the distinction between those and society.

There is enormous variation in the relative contributions of the different domains to explanations of particular diseases or disorders. Huntington’s disease has an established genetic cause and we need no psychological or societal explanations for its existence. However, devising optimal treatment does require understanding of the psychological and societal factors involved. The biopsychosocial model should remind us to consider a broad range of explanations, not oblige us to find one in every box. If we insist in advance that all domains must play a substantial explanatory role, we risk directing attention and research towards filling the boxes rather than understanding the problem.

It is sobering to read Engel write 50 years ago: “The proposed biopsychosocial model provides a blueprint for research, a framework for teaching, and a design for action in the real world of health care. Whether it is useful or not remains to be seen.”
