Do not judge a book by its cover. It's a saying we take figuratively. Though, it's most applicable when taken literally or literarily. I love to read, and I read voraciously. My literary taste might not be refined (pulp examples), but I can read and do enjoy what some consider to be the greats of the written word. I cannot consider myself a true connoisseur of literature, but I know enough that it's about content, it's about meaning. What we value most is what it's about. Of course, it matters how the story is crafted--put together in words, phrases, sentences, paragraphs, and chapters. Some are even considered masters of the craft. The protagonist in Paul Foster's 4321 says that Flaubert was the best at writing sentences **or something**.

How type is set on a page is less often considered. A well-balanced page layout is a thing of beauty, though not often recognized. Large enough margins to hold the book without your fingers covering the words, line spacing just right so sentences don't seem disconnected without crowding each other, and a font that is both readable and matches the demeanor of the book. **Add something about justification.** Just like convoluted or choppy phrasing, bad page layout can ruin the reading experience of the best of written products. The cover might not be that important, but how the words, headings, and pages look does carry considerable weight. Typesetting is a supportive art; it enhances the reading experience without distracting from it. Thus, when typesetting is done well, it becomes nearly invisible. You start to notice it when something is off or when it's just done poorly. Have you ever had a non-fiction book by some celebrity trying their hand at something they think is profound; a hard cover, quite thick, that just felt too light? I looks like a +400 page tome. Then you open it, and you realize that the margins are too wide, the font too large, and lines spaced too far apart (and often they used thick, though light, paper to add additional volume). Not only do you feel cheated (though afterwards relieved that it was actually a lot shorter than expected), you are also constantly distracted by the inappropriate typesetting of this book. You might not know what I am talking about. You might even think that I am exaggerating. Just pay close attention to the next couple of books you read or browse the non-fiction section for books by politicians, and you'll soon agree with me.

To round off this introduction, good typesetting is an art that should be respected and good prose, even in academic journal publications, deserves to look good on the page. That is why the formatting style propagated by the American Psychological Association is an aesthetic disgrace and an affront to human sensibility. There is just something horrendous and gut-wrenching when you have to transform your beautifully typeset manuscript to APA 7 when you submit it to an APA journal. Here are APA's four major typesetting horrors:

**Headings**
The heading structure is a special kind of ugly and infamous in its awkwardness. First of all, headings are the same font and size as the main body. Are they using Gutenberg's first movable type printing press with a single type size? APA uses five levels of headings with these same-sized letters:

- **Level one**: bold, centered, title case.
- **Level two**: bold, left-flushed, title case.
- **Level three**: bold, italicized, left-flushed, title case.
- **Level four:** bold, indented, title case, ending with a period. After which, the text continues on the same line, as though nothing odd has happened!
- **Level five**: same deal but now also in italics.
Nothing is good about this. Starting all major words with a capital letter looks awkward on a section heading that's a description and not really a title. There is no way to distinguish levels 2 from 3, and 4 from 5, unless you know the rules. And putting the heading in the main text is stretching the definition of the word heading beyond the limits of propriety. Using bold or italics is not bad per se, but it's not good either when wielded in such a careless and haphazard manner.

**Unnumbering**
This brings us to the next crime: numberless headings. There is something elegant to the efficient manner in which a writer can go through a narrative without breaking stride by referring to what has preceded, without having to repeat themselves, referring to what is to come later, without needing to provide a mini exposition on the spot. A "see Section …" and then a number is all that is needed in such a case, and pace and beauty are preserved. This is made almost impossible by the APA. As a writer, you are not allowed to number your headings. This is a senseless act of cruelty. I cannot fathom what would move a person to institute such a despotic rule. Ironically, allowing numbering would solve the confusion between heading levels 2 and 3, and 4 and 5.

**Double spacing**
Having to stretch your manuscript is like traveling back in time. Imagine you have your line distance set to a respectable 1.15, and then you stretch it to the required 2.0. Your paragraphs, nicely varying in length, creating a natural structure across the pages, that was both pleasing to look at and pleasant to read, are literally torn apart. Just so some stuffy professor can physically print your manuscript and write comments between the lines? Is there anybody alive who still does that? And then photocopies these annotated pages and sends these to the editor? If nothing else, we should stop this to save paper and make a modest contribution towards saving the environment.

**Flushed-left**
I like the word "justified"; I like the typographic alignment even more. Making the text flush with both the left and right margin creates structured pages of simple seeming beauty. This justification is achieved by three means: varying spaces between letters, varying spaces between words, moving the last part of the last word to the next line (i.e., hyphenation). When done poorly, typographic anomalies occur (e.g., large spaces between words, river effects). Proper justification is a technical art form, which we have luckily mastered and automated in microtypography. All of this must be new to the APA. They demand the flush left typographic alignment. A horror to behold and rightly called ragged-right.

**Conclusion**
It might seem trivial to care this much about typesetting. After all, as I said at the beginning, it's about content: about the words, not the way they look. But doesn't the quality of music depend on the instruments on which it is played? It's not like Vivaldi's The Four Seasons (or any other piece with a good melody for violin) sounds the same on a plywood violin and a Stradivarius. Though it plays a supportive role, form is part of meaning. The way text sits on a page affects how we read, how we think, how we feel about what's written. APA seems unaware of this, indifferent to it, or, as I suspect, maliciously subverting it. In their zeal for standardization, they've drained academic writing of any aesthetic dignity it might once have had.

It is a strange irony: the very discipline that studies perception, cognition, and emotion mandates a formatting style that offends all three. The result is not clarity, but sterility—a textual landscape so barren that even well-crafted arguments look bored with themselves. If Gutenberg's press had produced pages like these, I suspect the Renaissance would have been delayed by a century.

So yes, perhaps we shouldn't judge a book by its cover. But we can, and should, judge a discipline by its typography. And psychology, under APA 7, looks like it was typeset by someone who hates reading.
