Writing papers disgusts me. I don't mind writing; it even brings me joy, on occasion. I just loathe writing _papers_: creating formulaic academic manuscripts, the tedium of submitting them for publication, the endless revisions forced upon me by vindictive reviewers, until, at the end, when it is finally done and published, the only thing I feel is relief. I do it, because I'm forced to. If I didn't, my career as an academic would soon be over. But I do it reluctantly and need to share why I despise it so.

We are scientists and researchers. What we do is research. We try to figure stuff out: we explore, experiment, test predictions, and formulate explanations and theories. A paper is not the research. It is not what we thought, tried, and figured out. A paper is nothing more than a report: the report of the work that was done and the knowledge that was gained. As a report, the academic paper most resembles an autopsy report. The research is finished, the methods and data have been put to rest, and the researchers have moved on. In the autopsy report, the writers reconstruct events, establish means and circumstances, and speculate on the causes and implications. Of course, autopsy reports are invaluable. In forensics, however, they typically don't mistake what's what: it's about the body, and the report is just a medium to communicate a message.

There's just something obscene about researchers asking what the paper will look like or where we are going to submit it when discussing a project proposal. There is no corpse yet, and they are already selling its hide.

An autopsy report is pragmatic. The language should be clear; it should not contain (recognizable) errors, and the content should fit the purpose: _Cause of death: exsanguination due to laceration of the left femoral artery; estimated time of death: 22:40; no evidence of foul play._ It is not there to console the family, impress the coroner, or win a beauty prize. The same should go for papers (and their reviewers): readable prose, logical consistency, methods without obvious flaws, and relevance to the stated question. Anything beyond that slides into malpractice. It is dressing up a corpse for a funeral: ornate prose, theatrical "novelty," and significance lipstick. It is padding the file with irrelevant family history, such as gratuitous citations and performative literature tours to appease gatekeepers. It is pretending that reviewers make a meaningful contribution to the research when, at their most energetic, they rearrange flowers around a body they never examined alive. Peer review, as practiced, is terrible at detecting serious, covert errors or fraud; it mostly polices manners. And when it "improves" a paper, the improvement is cosmetic: tidier sentences, cleaner figures, one more robustness check posed like a corsage. None of that alters the cause of death or the chain of events that produced the body. It only makes the corpse presentable.

Writing papers is anachronistic. The modern paper descends from a 17th-century hack: the _Republic of Letters_, scholars trading correspondence across Europe. In March 1665, Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society, launched _Philosophical Transactions_; that same year, Paris saw _Journal des sçavans_. The mold was set: fixed issues, epistolary reports, priority claims. Print-era solutions to the scarcity of speed, access, and archiving. We kept the mold long after the constraints vanished. Page limits made sense when journals were literally printed; today, we still mail each other letters as PDFs. It should embarrass a community that enabled universal computation, digitization, global networks, and cloud storage that our knowledge still lives as static files. Most PDFs are scarcely better than posted offprints. As databases, paper corpora are a horror to search, aggregate, or synthesize. And correcting an error in "the literature" is a Sisyphean chore.

Papers don't match the timing, tempo, or architecture of research. Theory (development) is iterative and reusable; methods persist across projects and need not be tethered to any one theory; observation is linear and fleeting (you gather data once); results are snapshots among many admissible analyses; interpretation is a time-stamped judgment that should shift as new evidence and ideas arrive. The paper splices these mismatched rhythms into a single embalmed narrative. It is static, designed to look finished. To revise it meaningfully, you write another paper about the first, often years later and elsewhere. Corrections and retractions exist, but the culture around them is punitive or performative. An archive built from such objects cannot grow the way knowledge grows: branching, forking, backtracking, tightening around failures and partial successes. The record freezes a moment; the science moves on.

Papers do not naturally compose. You cannot plug one paper into another and get a better explanation. Citations gesture at lineage but rarely provide working inheritance. When we say "the literature," we mean a heap of stories about methods and results. Each story re-narrates prior work in miniature, each compressing the messy scaffolding of models, data, and decisions into a smooth paragraph. The best of these stories are admirable; most of them are indistinguishable. They resist synthesis because they are optimized for _telling_ rather than _connecting_.

I did not become a scientist to write papers. I think no one became a scientist because they wanted to write papers and try to get them past peer review of "prestigious" journals. We did not sign up to hone a genre. We signed up to explain, to predict, to model, to test, to build. The paper is supposed to be a report on that activity, not its raison d'être. If you want to know why science so often fails to synthesize—or why we keep rediscovering things we already know—start by looking at this culture that forces us to disassemble living inquiries into dead narratives. Stop writing papers. Not because writing is bad, but because we have lost sight of who we are and what we wanted to do when we became scientists.
